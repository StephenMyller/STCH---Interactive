
\subsection{Axiom of Extensionality}

\nbgpara{1}{}
Our underlying logic already provides the concept of identity. What remains to be determined is what makes two classes identical. We showed in the preceding section that identical classes must have the same members. But could two classes have exactly the same members and nevertheless be distinct? The logical rules alone do not settle this question. Our first set-theoretic axiom does.

\begin{axiom}[\textbf{\hspace{-0.12cm}Extensionality}]
\label{ax:extensionality}
For all classes \(A\) and \(B\), if \(A\) and \(B\) have exactly the same elements, then they are identical. In symbols:
\[
\forall A\forall B
\bigl(
\forall x(x\in A\leftrightarrow x\in B)
\to A=B
\bigr).
\]
\end{axiom}

In Gentzen derivations, we use \(\mathrm{Ext}\) to abbreviate the inference licensed by this axiom:

\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{\(\forall x(x\in A\leftrightarrow x\in B)\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\mathrm{Ext}\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(A=B\)}
\end{prooftree}

\nbgpara{2}{}
The axiom's contribution can be better appreciated by considering how it could be different. The name \emph{Extensionality} comes from the word \emph{extension}: the extension of a class consists of its members. An \emph{intensional} description, by contrast, concerns the property or condition by which those members are picked out.

Consider the class of all letters appearing in the word \emph{silent} and the class of all letters appearing in \emph{listen}. The descriptions differ, but they pick out exactly the same letters. By Extensionality, they determine one and the same class. The way we describe or enumerate a class's members is therefore not part of its identity; only the members themselves matter.


\nbgpara{3}{}
The Axiom of Extensionality also establishes an important property of subclasshood. We have already shown that subclasshood is reflexive and transitive. We can now establish a third property, called \emph{antisymmetry}.

\begin{proposition}[Antisymmetry of Subclasshood]
\label{prop:subclasshood-antisymmetric}
For all classes \(A\) and \(B\), if \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\subseteq A\), then \(A=B\). In symbols:
\[
(A\subseteq B\land B\subseteq A)\to A=B.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(A\) and \(B\) be arbitrary classes, and suppose that \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\subseteq A\). Let \(x\) be any object. Since \(A\subseteq B\), if \(x\in A\), then \(x\in B\). Conversely, since \(B\subseteq A\), if \(x\in B\), then \(x\in A\). Thus,
\[
x\in A\leftrightarrow x\in B.
\]
Since \(x\) was arbitrary, the two classes have exactly the same members. By the Axiom of Extensionality, \(A=B\).
\end{proof}

\begin{formalderivation}
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{\(A\subseteq B\)}
%
    \AxiomC{}
    \RightLabel{\(1\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in A\)}
%
    \RightLabel{\(\subseteq\)-E}
    \BinaryInfC{\(a\in B\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\to\)-I, \(1\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in A\to a\in B\)}
%
    \AxiomC{\(B\subseteq A\)}
%
    \AxiomC{}
    \RightLabel{\(2\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in B\)}
%
    \RightLabel{\(\subseteq\)-E}
    \BinaryInfC{\(a\in A\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\to\)-I, \(2\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in B\to a\in A\)}
%
    \RightLabel{\(\leftrightarrow\)-I}
    \BinaryInfC{\(a\in A\leftrightarrow a\in B\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\forall\)-I}
    \UnaryInfC{\(\forall x(x\in A\leftrightarrow x\in B)\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\mathrm{Ext}\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(A=B\)}
\end{prooftree}
\end{formalderivation}

Antisymmetry completes a familiar mathematical pattern. A relation is called a \emph{partial order} if it is reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric. Reflexivity says that every object stands in the relation to itself; transitivity says that the relation can be passed along a chain; and antisymmetry says that two objects standing in the relation to one another must be identical.

These three properties now hold for subclasshood. Thus, \(\subseteq\) is a partial order on classes.

The word \emph{partial} reflects the fact that a partial order need not compare every pair of objects. Unlike a \emph{total order}, it does not require that, for any two classes \(A\) and \(B\), either \(A\subseteq B\) or \(B\subseteq A\).

\nbgpara{4}{}
We can now combine the preceding proposition with a result from the previous section to obtain a complete criterion for the identity of classes.

\begin{proposition}[Extensional Criterion for Equality]
\label{prop:extensional-equality}
For all classes \(A\) and \(B\),
\[
A=B
\quad\text{iff}\quad
A\subseteq B\text{ and }B\subseteq A.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:identity-implies-mutual-subclasshood}, if \(A=B\), then \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\subseteq A\). Conversely, by Proposition~\ref{prop:subclasshood-antisymmetric}, if \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\subseteq A\), then \(A=B\). Together, these two implications establish the biconditional.
\end{proof}

\nbgpara{5}{}
This criterion gives us a fundamental proof strategy, called \emph{proof by double inclusion} or \emph{proof by mutual inclusion}: to establish \(A=B\), prove \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\subseteq A\) separately. We record the strategy as a derived rule.

\begin{derivedrule}[Mutual Inclusion]
\label{rule:mutual-inclusion}
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{\(A\subseteq B\)}
    \AxiomC{\(B\subseteq A\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\mathrm{MI}\)}
    \BinaryInfC{\(A=B\)}
\end{prooftree}
\end{derivedrule}



Unlike the derived subclass rules introduced earlier, Mutual Inclusion (\(\mathrm{MI}\)) depends on a non-logical axiom: Extensionality.


\nbgpara{6}{Uniqueness by Extensionality}
Suppose we specify a class by giving a condition \(\varphi(x)\) that characterizes exactly which objects belong to it. Could two different classes satisfy this same specification? If every member of either class must satisfy \(\varphi(x)\), and every object satisfying \(\varphi(x)\) must belong to both, then the two classes necessarily have the same members. Extensionality tells us they must be identical.

Importantly, this argument does not depend on what the condition \(\varphi(x)\) says. It gives us a general principle of uniqueness.

\begin{proposition}[Uniqueness of Class Specifications]
\label{prop:uniqueness-class-specification}
Let \(\varphi(x)\) be a fixed condition on \(x\) that does not depend on \(A\) or \(B\). If
\[
\forall x\bigl(x\in A\leftrightarrow\varphi(x)\bigr)
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\forall x\bigl(x\in B\leftrightarrow\varphi(x)\bigr),
\]
then \(A=B\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If \(A\) and \(B\) satisfy the given specifications, then for every object \(x\),
\[
x\in A\quad\text{iff}\quad\varphi(x)\quad\text{iff}\quad x\in B.
\]
Hence they have exactly the same members, and Extensionality gives \(A=B\).
\end{proof}

\begin{formalderivation}
Choose \(a\) fresh for the two specifications. We first derive:

\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{\(\forall x(x\in A\leftrightarrow\varphi(x))\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\forall\)-E}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in A\leftrightarrow\varphi(a)\)}
%
    \AxiomC{}
    \RightLabel{\(1\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in A\)}
%
    \RightLabel{\(\leftrightarrow\)-E}
    \BinaryInfC{\(\varphi(a)\)}
%
    \AxiomC{\(\forall x(x\in B\leftrightarrow\varphi(x))\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\forall\)-E}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in B\leftrightarrow\varphi(a)\)}
%
    \RightLabel{\(\leftrightarrow\)-E}
    \BinaryInfC{\(a\in B\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\to\)-I, \(1\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in A\to a\in B\)}
\end{prooftree}

Interchanging \(A\) and \(B\) gives the converse implication. Combining both directions, we obtain:

\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{\(\vdots\)}
    \noLine
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in A\to a\in B\)}
%
    \AxiomC{\(\vdots\)}
    \noLine
    \UnaryInfC{\(a\in B\to a\in A\)}
%
    \RightLabel{\(\leftrightarrow\)-I}
    \BinaryInfC{\(a\in A\leftrightarrow a\in B\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\forall\)-I}
    \UnaryInfC{\(\forall x(x\in A\leftrightarrow x\in B)\)}
    \RightLabel{\(\mathrm{Ext}\)}
    \UnaryInfC{\(A=B\)}
\end{prooftree}
\end{formalderivation}

This illustrates a general method for proving uniqueness: assume that two candidates satisfy a specification, then show that they must be identical.

\nbgpara{6}{Uniqueness and Existence}
There is a crucial limitation to this result. We have proved that \emph{at most one} class can satisfy a given membership condition, not that any such class exists. Our argument would still be valid if no class satisfied the condition at all.

This distinction will matter when we eventually introduce notation for classes specified by membership conditions. Before speaking of \emph{the} class satisfying a condition, we must establish its existence; Extensionality already guarantees its uniqueness. We therefore turn next to the question of which classes exist.
